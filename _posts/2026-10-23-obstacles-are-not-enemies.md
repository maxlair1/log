#### _That boulder on your running path might be there to keep you from jogging off the cliff. Moving the obstacle simply reveals the cliff. It takes climbing over it to realize you'd much rather climb than run._
---

### Introduction

Our family recently moved into a beautiful 1920s home. Along with the creaky floors, lead paint, and decades character, came a backyard that is roughly four times the size of our previous home's property.

I was quietly holding out for winter to save me from mowing a half-acre with a wimpy, electric push mower. Unfortunately, after seeing the property line between my neighbor's yard and ours become more and more obvious, I gave in.

I remember groveling, "man, I really wish I could wait another week or so... maybe borrow a riding mower. *This is going to take forever*". After all, that is my sacred time I could never get back.

Over the next (no joke) two and a half hours, I quite poorly mowed the overgrown grass. I needed to split it up into two sessions because my mower's battery only held a charge long enough for one-half of the yard.

However, something unexpected happened: I *loved* it. All that "wasted" time allowed reflection, skill-crafting, and creative problem-solving. I was also left with a sense of accomplishment that felt deserved.

Seems what I needed was not a bigger mower, a smaller backyard, or an early winter. It was a perspective adjustment.

I realized that I have a reflex to consider a lot of life's experiences like inconvenience. ==I've heard a common belief that life is full of simple tasks that have somehow become abstracted and complex. Like each thing we are meant to do has boiled down to a problem to be solved. As a result, we have over-engineered the everyday, and changed the way we relate to experiences.==

### Convenience

If you asked a friend about their life only to have them reply, *"it's been really convenient,"* you'd probably start asking who swapped your friend with this robotic doppelgänger, or assume they meant it sarcastically. However, consider a more realistic response: *"I've been getting a lot done lately."* How would simply completing tasks convey anything meaningful about their wellbeing? I'd argue that this is response is no better than a small-talk answer. It stops any conversation about our personal fulfillment dead in it's tracks. Even so, why is the emphasis on productivity or efficiency commonly used to communicate virtue? 

We must have our meals delivered straight to our doorstep instead of cooking. Heaven-forbid we think critically about politics, religion, or our passions before reaching for an AI chatbot. I'd start to sound like an alien in this work to recommend-- for any reason-- that you should make your own clothes. It seems like we are also all incapable of doing "nothing in particular". Perhaps life is meant to be filled with unavoidable pockets of downtime.  In the pursuit of efficiency we are simply becoming hollow. 

Imagine purchasing a brand-new video game you are excited to play, then immediately paying someone to beat it for you.

Society, capitalism, or work culture could be to blame, but we are still accountable for how we perceive our lives. Life cannot be genuinely enjoyed, if it is simply seen as a box of problems to be solved.

We are all too concerned with innovation and racing to the next big advancement. A relentless pursuit of productivity; Improved means to an unimproved end[^1]. Meanwhile, there are tangible, grounding experiences all around us that beg to be valued.

I'm not suggesting that we intentionally make life grueling, but in many ways, taking life as it is-- dripping with infinite inconvenience-- allows us the freedom to value the slow, "unproductive," and [sisyphean](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/Sisyphean) experiences. This mindset also redeems our times of waiting. It gives us daily transitions and a motivated search for small, spontaneous joys. Pausing feels more like living than suffocating. ==Life stops feeling like blinks of minuscule or insufficient pleasures, and becomes a spectrum of *happenings*.==

There is also a big difference between automation and routine. We as humans love to outsource non-essential tasks as often as possible, and we attempt to do this with anything that fits the bill. But sometimes we introduce bad friction

### Friction

Life is full of friction, but it is important to define what is *good friction*, and what is bad friction. Friction causes a slow down; It makes an action harder to complete. The misconception is that all slowing is a bad thing. 

I work as a User Experience Designer, so to me it is hard to imagine positive friction, as any obstacles are often considered a major red flag. Speed, ease-of-use, and (of course) convenience, are also intertwined into the UX field as

Friction in user interfaces might actually the perfect demonstration. Think about an app or website you have interacted with recently. Where has your experience improved due to an alert dialog or artificial delay? Instead, since the experiences that work as intended don't often stick out, try to remember a time when entering a phone number, uploading a file, or resetting a password was unnecessarily difficult.

I would guess that the difference between a good and bad experience was determined by the type of friction used (or removed) here.

For example, lot's of password creation forms require you "confirm"  your new password by entering it again, and requiring that the two match. I've never talked to anyone that thought this was the ideal friction to use here. Instead, what a lot of applications do is search for the root issue, and include an option to reveal the password. That way you can be sure of your input without being required to match twovalues that may or may not be completely hidden to you.

As far as this applies to the real world, I read an awesome [article by Monika Tomanek](https://medium.com/@resonance.heals/what-ux-design-taught-me-about-friction-pauses-and-why-not-every-obstacle-is-the-enemy-05462e46edd0) that bridged these two worlds together. She writes:

>"_I think that is why the idea feels so useful outside screens, too. Not all resistance in my life is pointless. Some of it is structure. Some of it protects me from choices that are easy in the moment and inconvenient later. A pause before replying. A limit before overcommitting. A little effort added in the place where impulse usually wins. Keeping a little distance between myself and something impulsive. Adding a rule before a choice, I know I make too quickly. Leaving enough time to think before saying yes. Sometimes they are simply good friction.  
A helpful pause. A small delay. A small layer between urge and action (that layer can change everything)._"[^2]

### How then can we enjoy the monotonous?

Don't get me wrong, it's not wrong to fight for [common graces](https://www.gotquestions.org/common-grace.html), progress, and making things easier. But perhaps we are pushing with a tad too much gusto. Our ambition to rid the world of friction has the wrong stuff in it's crosshairs.

King Solomon came to a similar conclusion. In the Book of Ecclesiastes (my favorite) When faced with the existential dread of endless toil, temporary joy, and a quickly approaching demise, he tries *all the things*. 

He indulges endlessly, yet still comes up unfulfilled. 

He denied flesh to the uttermost, and treasured knowledge, yet found those things are

He worked himself down to the bone, yet his successor squabbled his efforts in an instant, and any profit still added nothing to his soul.

Then in his God-given wisdom he determined:

> "*There is nothing better for a person than that he should eat and drink and **find enjoyment in his \[work\]**. This also, I saw, is from the hand of God...*"[^3]

The *work*-- easy, hard; good and bad-- is to be enjoyed. This means doing the dishes every night *could* be more efficient, or convenient, but ==something needs to adopt our work==.

### Conclusion

Since mowing a big yard taught me a little more about patience, delighting in monotony, and the dangerous over-correction humans have taken, I have been working on my perspective. Even small trips to fill up our water jug seem more lively. Breaks without a phone, looking out at moving grass seem to string together my life more vividly. It's exciting to learn this in (and right before) a major life stage, so I can orient myself between lasting joy in Christ, but beauty in the brokenness here and now.

This is our great challenge, to make the most of the overlooked moments in our lives-- not by reclaiming them in the name of efficiency, or documenting them until they feel meaningful, but really sitting with them; Being okay with the chores you must do, the project that was supposed to last a few days that has snow-balled to a month, or the yard that needs mowed. 

Even thought it will take *forever*

Even though it could be a bit *easier*

What do you have to get back to anyway, but this?
